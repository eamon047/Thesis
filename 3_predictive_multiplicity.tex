\section{Related Work}
\label{chap:predictive_multiplicity}

\subsection{Model Multiplicity in Machine Learning}

Model multiplicity refers to the existence of several models that perform
similarly on the same prediction task. It is often discussed through the
Rashomon effect: different models can achieve similar aggregate error while
representing the data in different ways
\citep{breiman2001statistical,fisher2019all}.
Aggregate evaluation may therefore make the models appear interchangeable even
when they do not make the same decisions for individual inputs.

This issue is also related to underspecification: a fixed training and
validation pipeline can produce predictors with similarly strong held-out
performance but different behavior \citep{damour2022underspecification}.

Predictive multiplicity focuses specifically on these differences in
predictions. In binary classification, \citet{marx2020predictive} study cases
where models with comparable error rates assign different labels to the same
individuals. Related work has extended this question to probabilistic
classification \citep{watsondaniels2023predictive} and has examined how model
multiplicity affects counterfactual explanations
\citep{pawelczyk2020counterfactual}. These studies use different prediction
outputs, but they share the distinction between average performance and the
stability of individual decisions.

This distinction also applies to ranking tasks. A link-prediction model does
not assign only one class label; it ranks candidate entities for a query. Two
models may obtain similar aggregate ranking accuracy while placing the correct
entity within the top-$K$ for different queries. Predictive multiplicity in
KGE-based link prediction concerns this query-level difference.

\subsection{Predictive Multiplicity in KGE-Based Link Prediction}

\citet{zhu2024predictive} introduced predictive multiplicity for KGE-based
link prediction. Their formulation compares a baseline model with competing
models whose aggregate Hits@$K$ values are close to that of the baseline. Let
$\mathcal{M}$ be a class of KGE models, let $M^*\in\mathcal{M}$ be the
baseline, and let $H_K(M)$ denote its aggregate Hits@$K$. The performance
difference is
\begin{align}
    D(M,M^*)=H_K(M^*)-H_K(M).
\end{align}
Given an error tolerance $\epsilon$, the corresponding $\epsilon$-level set is
\begin{align}
    S_\epsilon(M^*)
    =
    \{M\in\mathcal{M}\mid D(M,M^*)\leq\epsilon\}.
\end{align}
Models in this set can be close in aggregate performance while giving different
top-$K$ outcomes for individual testing items.

In the experiments of the original study, the baseline was selected through
hyperparameter optimization. The selected configuration was then trained with
different random seeds to obtain a sampled approximation of the
$\epsilon$-level set. The study used $\epsilon=0.01$ and limited this sampled
set to ten models \citep{zhu2024predictive}. This is the protocol used in that
study; the repeated-run comparison used in this thesis is defined separately
in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}.

Across six KGE methods and several benchmark datasets, the original study
reported conflicting top-$K$ outcomes for approximately 8--39\% of testing
items \citep{zhu2024predictive}. These results show that similar aggregate
accuracy does not guarantee the same query-level decisions. The principal
reported measures summarize multiplicity over an entire testing set, however,
so they do not show how the conflicts are distributed across relations or
prediction sides.

\subsection{Measures and Mitigation in the Original Study}

The original study adapts ambiguity and discrepancy, previously used in
classification research \citep{marx2020predictive,watsondaniels2023predictive},
to top-$K$ link prediction. Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the testing-item set. Each
item $\tau=(q,e)$ consists of a query $q$ and its held-out correct entity $e$.
For a model $M$, let $T_K(M,\tau)$ be one if $e$ is ranked within the top-$K$
for $q$, and zero otherwise. The conflict indicator between $M$ and the
baseline is
\begin{align}
    \Delta(M,\tau)
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[T_K(M,\tau)\neq T_K(M^*,\tau)\right].
\end{align}
It records a change in the binary top-$K$ outcome, rather than a difference
between the complete candidate rankings.

Ambiguity is the proportion of testing items for which at least one competing
model conflicts with the baseline:
\begin{align}
    \alpha_\epsilon(M^*)
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}
    \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}
    \max_{M\in S_\epsilon(M^*)}
    \Delta(M,\tau).
\end{align}
It asks how widely the possibility of a changed prediction is distributed
over the testing set. Discrepancy instead measures the largest disagreement
produced by any one competing model:
\begin{align}
    \delta_\epsilon(M^*)
    =
    \max_{M\in S_\epsilon(M^*)}
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}
    \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}
    \Delta(M,\tau).
\end{align}
Thus, ambiguity can combine conflicts produced by different competing models,
whereas discrepancy concerns the single model with the greatest disagreement.
Both measures use all items in $\mathcal{T}$ in the original definitions.
They are not error rates: a conflict may arise because a competing model turns
a baseline miss into a hit or because it turns a baseline hit into a miss.

The same study also investigated voting as a mitigation method. Majority,
Borda, and range voting aggregate the candidate rankings produced by several
models into one ranking at test time. The reported results show that ranking
aggregation can reduce predictive conflicts, although majority voting can also
reduce Hits@$K$ \citep{zhu2024predictive}. This thesis does not develop or
evaluate a new voting method; it studies where multiplicity appears before
such mitigation.

\subsection{Research Gap and Position of This Thesis}

The existing KGE study establishes that predictive multiplicity occurs, but a
single ambiguity or discrepancy value for a model--dataset setting does not
identify where the instability occurs. In particular, it does not show whether
conflicts are distributed evenly across relations, whether head and tail
prediction behave differently, or whether multiplicity varies with relation
patterns.

This thesis addresses this gap through a relation-level empirical analysis. It
uses repeated runs of RotatE and TransE and groups their top-10 outcomes by
relation and, where needed, by prediction side. It then compares relation-level
accuracy and multiplicity across mapping type, inverse, and symmetry. The aim
is to locate and describe prediction instability, rather than to propose a new
KGE model or a new mitigation method.

The analysis is based on the concepts of ambiguity and discrepancy above, but
it is not presented as an exact reproduction of the original paper's complete
$\epsilon$-level-set protocol. Chapter~\ref{chap:framework} defines the current
comparable repeated-run setting, the relation-specific eligible subset, and the
relation-level versions of the two measures.
