\section{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

A knowledge graph represents a fact as a triple consisting of two objects and
the relation between them. For example, the fact that Paris is the capital of
France can be written as \texttt{(Paris, capital\_of, France)}. Objects such as
Paris and France are called entities, while \texttt{capital\_of} is the
relation connecting them. Many such triples form a graph of connected facts.
Knowledge graphs are used in applications such as semantic search,
recommendation, and question answering. More recently, knowledge graphs have
also been combined with large language models in retrieval-augmented
generation, where structured knowledge can be retrieved as external
information during inference \citep{pan2024roadmap}. However, real-world
knowledge graphs are often incomplete, and some valid links between entities
are missing. Link prediction aims to recover these missing links
\citep{ji2022survey}.

Knowledge graph embedding (KGE) models perform link prediction by learning
vector representations of entities and relations. For a query with a missing
head or tail entity, a KGE model assigns scores to candidate entities and ranks
them according to these scores. Model performance is usually evaluated using
aggregate ranking metrics. Hits@10, for example, records whether the correct
entity is ranked among the top ten candidates and averages this outcome over
the test queries. This metric summarizes overall accuracy, but it does not show
whether two models with similar scores succeed on the same queries. Two models
can therefore obtain similar Hits@10 values while making different top-10
decisions for individual queries.

This type of query-level instability is referred to as predictive
multiplicity. Previous work introduced predictive multiplicity for KGE-based
link prediction and showed that models with comparable overall performance can
produce different predictions for the same query
\citep{zhu2024predictive}. That study summarized multiplicity mainly at the
global model level. This leaves open whether prediction instability affects
all relations similarly or is greater for some groups of relations than for
others.

The general aim of this thesis is to determine whether query-level prediction
instability, which is not visible in overall accuracy, is evenly distributed
or concentrated in identifiable groups of queries. We investigate whether such
groups can be identified from the relations used in the queries. To examine
this question experimentally, we use FB15k-237 and analyze repeated runs of
RotatE and TransE separately. Within each model family, the runs use the same
configuration and differ only in random seed. We classify relations in three
ways, referred to as relation patterns. Mapping type distinguishes one-to-one,
one-to-many, many-to-one, and many-to-many connections. Inverse relations
describe the same connection in opposite directions, while symmetric relations
hold in both directions. We compare relation-level Hits@10 and prediction
disagreement across these classifications. The results show that relation-based
groups differ in prediction stability. The clearest differences appear for
mapping type and depend on which side is predicted. A small group of relations
with clear inverse partners has lower multiplicity, while symmetry shows no
clear association. These findings suggest that overall accuracy can hide
systematic differences in stability across groups of queries.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 introduces
knowledge graphs, KGE models, link prediction evaluation, and the relation
patterns considered in this thesis. Chapter 3 reviews model multiplicity in
machine learning and previous work on predictive multiplicity in KGE-based
link prediction. Chapter 4 defines the relation-level analysis design and the
metrics used in this thesis. Chapter 5 presents the experimental setup and
results. Finally, Chapter 6 summarizes the findings, discusses the limitations
of the current analysis, and outlines directions for future work.
