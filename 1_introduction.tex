\section{Introduction}

We live in a world full of uncertainty, such as climate change and market fluctuation. 
People often have to make decisions in such environment, based on their limited information from the past. 
\textit{Sequential decision-making under uncertainty} is a field that studies how to optimize the decision-making process in such an uncertain environment.
The \textit{multi-armed bandit (MAB)} is a model in this field, which is often illustrated by a gambler playing slot machines. 
It has been widely applied in practical scenarios, such as dynamic pricing \citep{misra2019dynamic}, clinical trials \citep{durand2018contextual} and recommendation systems \citep{zhou2017large}. 
In this problem, the player has to choose one arm among multiple arms and incurs the loss from the chosen arm only. 
The difficulty of this problem arises from the ``partial feedback'' nature, where the player is only able to observe the loss from the chosen arm. 
Formally, at each round $t\in\qty[T]=\qty{1,2,\dots,T}$
for
%from
time horizon $T$, the player chooses an arm $I_t$ out of
%a set of
$K$ arms. 
The loss vector $\ell_t=\qty(\ell_{t,1},\ell_{t,2},\dots,\ell_{t,K})^\top\in\qty[0,1]^K$ is determined by the environment and the player could only observe the incurred loss $\ell_{t,I_t}$ from the chosen arm. 
The player's objective is to minimize his/her cumulative loss over all rounds. 
As a result, the player faces an \textit{exploration-exploitation trade-off}: whether to choose the arm that currently appears optimal or to explore the other arms that have potential to become truly optimal. The performance of the player is measured by the pseudo-regret, which describes the gap between the cumulative loss of the player and of the optimal arm fixed in hindsight.
% defined as $\mathcal{R}(T)=\mathbb{E}\qty[\sum_{t=1}^T \ell_{t, I_t}] - \min_{i \in [K]} \mathbb{E}\qty[\sum_{t=1}^T \ell_{t, i}]$.

Stochastic setting \citep{lai1985asymptotically,auer2002finite} and adversarial setting \citep{auer2002nonstochastic,audibert2009minimax} are two fundamental formulations of the environment to determine loss vectors.
In the stochastic setting, the losses $\ell_{t,i}$ from arm $i$ are i.i.d.~from an unknown but fixed distribution over $[0,1]$
with expectation $\mu_i$.
The suboptimality gap is expressed by $\Delta_i=\mu_i - \mu_{i^*}$
for the optimal arm $i^*\in{\arg\min}_{i\in\qty[K]}\mu_i$.
The  optimal (problem-dependent) regret bound given these gaps is known to be $\sum_{i : \Delta_i > 0} O\qty(\frac{\log T}{\Delta_i})$ \citep{lai1985asymptotically}, which can be achieved by several policies such as UCB \citep{auer2002finite,cappe2013kullback} and Thompson sampling \citep{kaufmann2012thompson,pmlr-v31-agrawal13a,riou2020bandit}, some of which further improve the regret bound by incorporating the dependence on the loss distribution itself.

In the adversarial setting, the loss vectors $\ell_t$ are not assumed to follow any specific distribution, and the environment may choose them based on the history of the decisions. For this setting, the optimal regret bound $O(\sqrt{KT})$ \citep{auer2002nonstochastic} is achieved by some policies, including a family of policies called the Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) with appropriate regularization functions \citep{audibert2009minimax,zimmert2019connections}.

One limitation of FTRL is that the arm-selection probability $w_t$ is required to be explicitly computed and stored in each round, which requires to solve an optimization problem involving a regularizer.
This becomes problematic in some complex settings, and the framework of Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader (FTPL)
is a promising candidate to overcome this limitation.
FTPL greedily chooses an arm with the minimum cumulative estimated loss with some random perturbation
and does not require to solve an optimization problem nor store the vector $w_t$.
These properties have made FTPL effective across various online learning and bandit problems,
including combinatorial semi-bandits~\citep{JMLR:v17:15-091}, online learning with non-linear losses~\citep{dudik2020oracle}, and MDP bandits~\citep{dai2022follow}, though the regret guarantees of FTPL are often somewhat weaker than FTRL.

From the theoretical viewpoint, FTPL has also been studied for the standard MAB in terms of the duality between FTRL and FTPL,
and
\citet{kim2019optimality} raised an open problem that FTPL achieving $O(\sqrt{KT})$ regret (if exists) would have perturbations following Fr\'{e}chet-type distributions.
As a solution to this conjecture, it has recently been proved that FTPL with Fr\'{e}chet perturbation with $\alpha=2$ achieves the BOBW guarantee \citep{pmlr-v201-honda23a},
which was later extended to more general Fr\'{e}chet-type distributions with some mild conditions \citep{pmlr-v247-lee24a}.

From the practical viewpoint, however,
the advantage of FTPL for the standard MAB has been somewhat limited
compared with the clear computational advantage of FTPL for complex problems.
The reason is that FTPL and FTRL generally need an unbiased (or low-bias) estimator for the loss vector $\ell_t$ for each round.
In FTRL,
the Importance-Weighted (IW) estimator is often used,
which is an unbiased estimator for $\ell_t$
using the inverse of the arm-selection probability $w_{t,i}^{-1}$.
On the other hand in FTPL, $w_{t}$ is not explicitly computed and the IW estimator becomes unavailable.

To address this problem, \citet{JMLR:v17:15-091} proposed a technique called Geometric Resampling (GR) to obtain an unbiased estimator
for $w_{t,i}^{-1}$, but
its computational cost is $O(K^2)$ per round in average.
On the other hand, $w_t$ for Tsallis-INF (i.e., FTRL with Tsallis-entropy regularizer) can be efficiently computed by, e.g., Newton's method,

Though the required number of iterations to keep the theoretical guarantee is formally unknown,
it is empirically known that $O(1)$ iterations is sufficient.
Therefore, the entire computational cost of Tsallis-INF per round becomes $O(K)$ in practice, which becomes more efficient
than FTPL with $O(K^2)$ cost.

\paragraph{Details of FTPL Literature}
While originally proposed by \citet{hannan1957approximation}, FTPL gained significant attention for its computational efficiency and simplicity after \citet{kalai2005efficient} introduced it in the context of linear optimization. 
These properties have made FTPL effective across various online learning and bandit settings, including combinatorial semi-bandits~\citep{JMLR:v17:15-091}, online learning with non-linear losses~\citep{dudik2020oracle}, and MDP bandits~\citep{dai2022follow}.
Although variants of FTPL have been developed to improve computational efficiency by avoiding direct optimization, many either sacrifice theoretical performance compared to FTRLs or become computationally expensive when only partial information is available.
For example, an earlier efficient FTPL algorithm was shown to suffer $O(T^{2/3})$ regret~\citep{awerbuch2004adaptive}, while another achieving $O(\sqrt{KT\log K})$ regret required $O(T^2)$ numerical operations per round~\citep{poland2005fpl}.
This inefficiency in bandit settings comes from the nature of FTPL, which avoids explicit optimizations to compute importance weights in closed form.
To address this issue, \citet{JMLR:v17:15-091} proposed GR in the line of combinatorial semi-bandits, whose reduction to MAB achieves $O(\sqrt{KT\log K})$ regret with $O(K^2)$ numerical operations per round.
Further refinements by \citet{pmlr-v201-honda23a} and \citet{pmlr-v247-lee24a} showed that FTPL with GR can achieve optimal $O(\sqrt{KT})$ regret and even BOBW guarantees for MAB problems when using appropriately chosen perturbation distributions.
However, in standard MAB problems, GR often incurs a higher computational cost than FTRL algorithms, despite FTRL requiring the solution of a convex optimization problem each round. 
This observation contrasts with combinatorial bandit settings, where GR was initially introduced, and motivates the design of a more efficient resampling method for FTPL.

In this algorithm, we use a value-swapping technique, which enables resampling from the conditional distribution of the perturbation distribution with almost no increase in computation time. Since value-swapping does not rely on the intrinsic properties of the perturbation distribution, CGR~I is applicable to any perturbation distributions. 

% Compared to CGR~I, CGR~II resamples from a more constrained conditional distribution, further increasing the probability of termination in each round. Since CGR~II has to resample from the conditional distribution, it requires the knowledge of the specific form of cumulative distribution function and its inverse. With the guarantee of the expected times of resampling, CGR~II also ensures that the expected times of resampling given the chosen arm is $O\qty(K)$. While CGR~II-unbiased also gives an unbiased estimator for $w_{t,i}^{-1}$, CGR~II-biased utilizes a setting of deterministic bound, which is called maximum resampling, introducing some bias to the estimator. To guarantee that the bias doesn't hurt the regret too much, we constrain the perturbations to follow Fr\'{e}chet distributions with shape $2$. Under this setting, our analysis shows that the bias contributes only an additional $K\log T$ term to the regret with a leading factor of $1$.
