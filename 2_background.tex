\section{Background}
\label{chap:background}

\subsection{Knowledge Graphs and Link Prediction}

A knowledge graph represents facts as directed relational triples
\citep{ji2022survey}. Let $\mathcal{E}$ be the set of entities and
$\mathcal{R}$ the set of relations. A knowledge graph can be written as
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{G}
    \subseteq
    \mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{E}.
\end{align}
Each triple $(h,r,t)\in\mathcal{G}$ contains a head entity $h$, a relation
$r$, and a tail entity $t$. Figure~\ref{fig:example-knowledge-graph}
illustrates this structure. For example, the edge from \emph{Mount Everest}
to \emph{Mountain} labeled \emph{instance of} corresponds to one triple.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{figures/chap2/map.pdf}
    \caption{An example knowledge graph. Nodes represent entities, and each
    labeled directed edge corresponds to a relational triple.}
    \label{fig:example-knowledge-graph}
\end{figure}

Since a knowledge graph may be incomplete, link prediction attempts to recover
missing entities from incomplete triples. A triple $(h,r,t)$ gives two query
forms:
\begin{align}
    q^{\mathrm{head}}=(?,r,t),
    \qquad
    q^{\mathrm{tail}}=(h,r,?).
\end{align}
The first query asks for the missing head entity, while the second asks for the
missing tail entity. Candidate entities from $\mathcal{E}$ are inserted into
the missing position, and the resulting triples are ranked by the model.

\subsection{Knowledge Graph Embedding Models}

Knowledge graph embedding (KGE) models assign learned low-dimensional
representations to entities and relations. A scoring function uses these
representations to measure the plausibility of a triple. The models used in
this thesis learn their representations by comparing observed training triples
with sampled alternatives. For link prediction, the same scoring function is
used to rank the candidate entities. This thesis considers TransE and RotatE,
which model relations using translation and rotation, respectively. Their
geometric operations are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:kge-geometries}.

\begin{figure}[H]

    \hfill
    \includegraphics[width=0.40\textwidth]{figures/chap2/RotatE.pdf}
    \caption{Geometric interpretation of TransE (left) and RotatE (right).
    TransE translates the head embedding by the relation embedding, whereas
    RotatE applies the relation as a rotation in complex space. In both
    diagrams, $d$ is the distance between the transformed head embedding and
    the tail embedding; a smaller distance gives a higher model score.}
    \label{fig:kge-geometries}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{TransE}

TransE represents entities and relations as vectors in a real-valued space and
models a relation as a translation from the head entity to the tail entity
\citep{bordes2013translating}. For a plausible triple,
$\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{r}$ should be close to $\mathbf{t}$. The left part of
Figure~\ref{fig:kge-geometries} shows this translation. Using the $L_2$
distance adopted in the current experiments, the score is
\begin{align}
    s_{\mathrm{TransE}}(h,r,t)
    =
    -\left\|\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{t}\right\|_2.
\end{align}
A smaller distance therefore gives a higher score.

\subsubsection{RotatE}

RotatE represents entities in a complex-valued space and models each relation
as an element-wise rotation \citep{sun2019rotate}. Each component of the
relation embedding has unit modulus. A plausible triple therefore satisfies
$\mathbf{h}\circ\mathbf{r}\approx\mathbf{t}$, where $\circ$ denotes
element-wise complex multiplication. The right part of
Figure~\ref{fig:kge-geometries} illustrates this operation for one complex
dimension. Under the $L_2$ distance used in the current experiments, the score
is
\begin{align}
    s_{\mathrm{RotatE}}(h,r,t)
    =
    -\left\|\mathbf{h}\circ\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{t}\right\|_2.
\end{align}
As in TransE, a smaller distance produces a higher score.

\subsection{Link Prediction Evaluation}

A KGE model evaluates a link-prediction query by scoring every candidate
entity. For a tail-prediction query $(h,r,?)$, each candidate $e\in\mathcal{E}$
forms the triple $(h,r,e)$. The candidates are ranked from the highest score to
the lowest. Head prediction follows the same procedure for triples $(e,r,t)$.

Two ranking settings are commonly reported in KGE evaluation
\citep{bordes2013translating}. In the raw setting, the model ranks the correct
entity against all candidate entities. In the filtered setting, other
candidates that form known true triples are removed before the rank of the
held-out correct entity is computed. This avoids penalizing the model when
another correct answer is ranked above the held-out answer. The experiments in
this thesis use filtered evaluation.

Let $\mathcal{T}$ be a set of evaluated query--answer pairs, and let
$\operatorname{rank}(\tau)$ be the filtered rank of the correct entity for
$\tau\in\mathcal{T}$. Hits@10 is
\begin{align}
    \operatorname{Hits@10}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}
    \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[\operatorname{rank}(\tau)\leq 10\right].
\end{align}
It is the proportion of evaluated items for which the correct entity is ranked
within the top ten. Hits@10 summarizes accuracy across a set of items, but it
does not show which items are solved by each model. Two models can therefore
have similar Hits@10 while succeeding on different queries.
Chapter~\ref{chap:predictive_multiplicity} discusses this distinction in
relation to predictive multiplicity.

\subsection{Relation Patterns in Knowledge Graph Embeddings}

Relations in a knowledge graph can be described by recurring structural
patterns. KGE studies use these patterns to analyze what kinds of triples a
scoring function can represent. Two common descriptions are cardinality-based
mapping types and logical patterns such as symmetry, antisymmetry, inversion,
and composition \citep{bordes2013translating,sun2019rotate,zhu2024rotation}.
This thesis uses \emph{relation patterns} as an umbrella term for these
descriptions. These terms describe graph structure; their association with
predictive multiplicity is examined empirically in
Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}.

\subsubsection{Mapping Type}

Mapping type describes whether the head and tail sides of a relation tend to
connect one or many entities \citep{bordes2013translating}. The four common
categories are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $1$-$1$: one head is associated with one tail, and vice versa;
    \item $1$-$N$: one head can be associated with multiple tails;
    \item $M$-$1$: multiple heads can be associated with one tail;
    \item $M$-$N$: multiple entities can occur on both sides.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:mapping-type-schematic} gives an example of each category.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/chap4/sum.pdf}
    \caption{Examples of the four mapping types. Nodes on the left and right
    represent head and tail entities, respectively, and each edge represents
    an observed triple under the relation shown in that panel.}
    \label{fig:mapping-type-schematic}
\end{figure}

Mapping type must be read together with the prediction side. For a $1$-$N$
relation, tail prediction targets the side classified as many. For an $M$-$1$
relation, head prediction targets the side classified as many. The averages
and threshold used to assign these categories in this thesis are defined in
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}.

\subsubsection{Inverse Relations}

Two relations are inverses when they describe the same connection in opposite
directions \citep{sun2019rotate,zhu2024rotation}. If $r_1$ and $r_2$ form an
inverse pair, a triple $(h,r_1,t)$ corresponds to $(t,r_2,h)$. For example,
\emph{parent of} and \emph{child of} reverse both the direction of the edge and
the relation label. This is a general relation-level concept.
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework} later defines how the present study measures
partial inverse support in the training graph.

\subsubsection{Symmetry}

A relation is symmetric when reversing its two entities preserves the same
relation \citep{sun2019rotate,zhu2024rotation}. In other words, if
$(h,r,t)$ holds, then $(t,r,h)$ also holds. Relations such as \emph{sibling of}
can have this form. Symmetry can be viewed as reversal under the same relation,
whereas the inverse case above uses a different relation as the reverse
partner. The empirical symmetry measure used in this thesis is given in
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}.

\subsubsection{Other Relation Patterns}

Antisymmetry and relation composition are also studied in KGE research
\citep{sun2019rotate,zhu2024rotation}. For an antisymmetric relation, two
distinct entities cannot be connected by that relation in both directions.
Composition concerns a path of relations: if $(x,r_1,y)$ and $(y,r_2,z)$ hold,
their combination may imply $(x,r_3,z)$. The order of $r_1$ and $r_2$ may also
matter. Transitivity is a special case in which the same relation takes the
roles of $r_1$, $r_2$, and $r_3$.

The present study focuses on mapping type, inverse relations, and symmetry.
Composition and other path-based patterns require an analysis of relation
sequences and are outside the current relation-level design. The selection and
operational definitions of the three analyzed patterns are described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}.
