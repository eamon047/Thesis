\section{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The experiments use FB15k-237, which contains 14,541 entities and 237
relations. Its training, validation, and test sets contain 272,115, 17,535,
and 20,466 triples, respectively. We consider two KGE model families, RotatE
and TransE. They provide two different model settings for checking whether the
observed relation-level patterns are specific to one embedding model.

For each model family, we use the training configuration selected through the
hyperparameter optimization in the original study
\citep{zhu2024predictive}. One run trained with this configuration is fixed as
the reference model, and additional models are trained with the same
configuration and different random seeds. Let \(H_0\) be the filtered Hits@10
of the reference model and \(H_i\) the filtered Hits@10 of a candidate model. A
candidate is included in the comparable model set when
\begin{align}
    |H_i-H_0| < \epsilon,
    \qquad \epsilon=0.001.
\end{align}
This process is repeated until the model outputs required for the multiplicity
analysis have been collected. Figure~\ref{fig:experimental-workflow} summarizes
this procedure. The performance condition keeps the comparison focused on
models with similar aggregate link-prediction performance, while the different
random seeds allow their query-level decisions to vary. The reference run is
fixed before the relation-level analysis and provides an anchor for defining
this performance neighborhood. The aim is to compare query-level decisions
within that neighborhood, rather than to estimate the best attainable
performance of either model family.

All link-prediction results are evaluated under the filtered setting, and
aggregate performance is reported using Hits@10. For each relation,
we report the relation-level Hits@10, ambiguity(\(\alpha\)), and 
discrepancy(\(\delta\)) defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}. The main 
figures and grouped comparisons retain relations with at least ten test triples
(\(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\)); relations below this threshold are not
used in the displayed comparisons.

\subsection{Mapping Type Analysis}
\label{sec:mapping_type_analysis}

Mapping type is the first relation-level structural factor considered in this
chapter. As defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}, it categorizes a relation
according to whether its head side and tail side behave as one or many in the
training graph. Because mapping type distinguishes whether the predicted side
is one- or many-valued in the training graph, it provides a relation-level
factor for comparing predictive multiplicity.

\subsubsection{Motivation for Side-Aware Analysis}

For a relation \(r\), tail prediction asks \((h,r,?)\), while head prediction
asks \((?,r,t)\). In the usual link-prediction evaluation, both query types are
part of the same relation-level result. A combined relation-level view therefore
computes one set of metrics for each relation after merging head-prediction and
tail-prediction queries.

This combined view is not fully aligned with how mapping type is defined.
Mapping type assigns the one-or-many property to a specific side of a relation.
For a \(1\)-\(N\) relation, the structurally many side appears in tail
prediction \((h,r,?)\): after the head entity and relation are fixed, multiple
tail entities may be plausible. The same relation does not have the same
structure on the head-prediction side \((?,r,t)\). Conversely, for an
\(M\)-\(1\) relation, the many side appears in head prediction. Therefore,
merging head and tail queries mixes the side where the mapping structure creates
more candidate ambiguity with the opposite side of the same relation.

If the two sides are combined, the result may look weaker because the difficult
side and the opposite side are averaged together. For this reason, the following
analysis reports tail prediction and head prediction separately. The combined
view is left to the appendix as a supporting check.

\subsubsection{RotatE Results}

The RotatE results are reported separately for tail prediction and head
prediction. Each figure groups relations by mapping type and reports
relation-level Hits@10, ambiguity \(\alpha\), and discrepancy \(\delta\).
Hits@10 gives the relation-level predictive performance, while \(\alpha\) and
\(\delta\) are the two multiplicity measures used in this chapter.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/chap5/mapping-type/rotate-tail.pdf}
    \caption{RotatE mapping-type results for tail prediction under
    \(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\). The red line marks the
    mean over all retained relations.}
    \label{fig:mapping-rotate-tail}
\end{figure}

The RotatE tail-prediction results are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:mapping-rotate-tail}, which presents the clearest contrast
among the four mapping types.
The \(1\)-\(N\) group has the lowest Hits@10 and the highest
\(\alpha\) and \(\delta\). Its tail predictions are therefore less accurate and
show more hit-or-miss disagreement across the repeated RotatE runs. The
\(M\)-\(1\) group is at the other end of the comparison: it has the highest
Hits@10 and the lowest \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\). Taken together, these results
suggest that predictive multiplicity varies across mapping types in RotatE tail
prediction.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/chap5/mapping-type/rotate-head.pdf}
    \caption{RotatE mapping-type results for head prediction under
    \(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\). The red line marks the
    mean over all retained relations.}
    \label{fig:mapping-rotate-head}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:mapping-rotate-head} shows a different pattern for head
prediction. The \(M\)-\(1\) group now has the lowest Hits@10 and the highest
\(\alpha\) and \(\delta\), while the \(1\)-\(N\) group has the highest Hits@10
and the lowest multiplicity measures. The relation group with lower accuracy
and higher multiplicity therefore changes when the prediction side changes.

\subsubsection{TransE Results}

The same side-aware comparison is also applied to TransE. The purpose of this
comparison is to check whether the directional pattern observed for RotatE also
appears under a different KGE model family.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/chap5/mapping-type/transe-tail.pdf}
    \caption{TransE mapping-type results for tail prediction under
    \(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\). The red line marks the
    mean over all retained relations.}
    \label{fig:mapping-transe-tail}
\end{figure}

Although TransE is a different model family, its tail-prediction result shows
the same contrast between \(1\)-\(N\) and \(M\)-\(1\). In
Figure~\ref{fig:mapping-transe-tail}, the \(1\)-\(N\) group again has the
lowest Hits@10 and the highest \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\), whereas the
\(M\)-\(1\) group has the highest Hits@10 and the lowest multiplicity measures.
The lower accuracy and higher instability of the \(1\)-\(N\) tail side are
therefore observed in both models.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/chap5/mapping-type/transe-head.pdf}
    \caption{TransE mapping-type results for head prediction under
    \(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\). The red line marks the
    mean over all retained relations.}
    \label{fig:mapping-transe-head}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:mapping-transe-head} completes the same reversal for head
prediction. The \(M\)-\(1\) group has the lowest Hits@10 and the highest
\(\alpha\) and \(\delta\). Compared with this group, the \(1\)-\(N\) group has
much higher Hits@10 and lower multiplicity measures. This agrees with the
RotatE head-prediction result.

A possible explanation is the set of constraints created by the training
triples. For a \(1\)-\(N\) relation, one fixed \((h,r)\) pair is connected to
several tail entities. Different training runs can represent or rank these
targets differently while retaining similar overall performance. This may lead
to higher \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\).

For an \(M\)-\(1\) relation, the constraints on tail prediction have a
different form. Triples such as \((h_1,r,t)\), \((h_2,r,t)\), and
\((h_3,r,t)\) provide several training constraints involving the same tail
entity, but each constraint belongs to a different tail query. The many-valued
structure is therefore on the head side rather than on the predicted tail side.
The same reasoning applies in reverse to head prediction, where a fixed
\((r,t)\) pair in an \(M\)-\(1\) relation is connected to multiple possible
head entities.

Under filtered evaluation, other known true answers are removed before the
candidate ranking is evaluated. The observed difference is therefore not
simply caused by known answers competing directly in the candidate list. The
four figures suggest that the constraints learned from the many side of
a relation leave more room for repeated runs to produce different ranking
decisions. This interpretation is consistent with the observed results, but
the current experiment does not test this training mechanism separately.

Overall, RotatE and TransE show the same side-dependent pattern on FB15k-237.
For \(1\)-\(N\) and \(M\)-\(1\) relations, predicting the side classified as
many is associated with lower accuracy and higher predictive multiplicity than
predicting the opposite side. This result suggests that the relation between
mapping type and predictive multiplicity depends on which side is predicted.

The exact grouped means for all four mapping types are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:mapping-type-tables}.

\subsection{Inverse Relation Analysis}
\label{sec:inverse_analysis}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

When two relations form an inverse pair, they describe the connection between
the same entities in opposite directions. Training edges under the two
relations can therefore provide paired structural evidence: one relation
supports the connection from the head to the tail, while its inverse partner
supports the reversed connection. Such evidence may constrain the relation
representations learned under different random seeds and make their ranking
decisions more stable.

The analysis uses the inverse clarity defined in
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}. This score measures whether the strongest mutual
inverse partner of a relation is clearly separated from its second-best
partner. A high value therefore indicates both substantial reverse support and
a clearly identified inverse partner. For readability, the figures in this
section use \emph{inverse score} as a shorter name for inverse clarity.

\subsubsection{Results}

Figure~\ref{fig:inverse-spearman} reports the global Spearman correlations
between the inverse score and the three relation-level evaluation metrics. If
increasing inverse scores provided a general stability benefit, the score would
be expected to have a positive association with Hits@10 and negative
associations with \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\). The observed correlations do not
show this pattern. For RotatE and TransE, the correlations with Hits@10 are
\(-0.2796\) and \(-0.2819\), respectively. The correlations with \(\alpha\)
and \(\delta\) are positive and range from \(0.2263\) to \(0.2427\). Thus, the
global results do not support a monotonic improvement in accuracy or stability
as the inverse score increases.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/chap5/inverse/spearman.pdf}
    \caption{Spearman correlations between inverse clarity and relation-level
    Hits@10, \(\alpha\), and \(\delta\) under
    \(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\).}
    \label{fig:inverse-spearman}
\end{figure}

The score-group comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:inverse-score-groups} shows that
the highest score range behaves differently from the global trend. Among the
182 retained relations, 78 have an inverse score of zero, 99 have a score in
the interval \((0,0.5]\), and five have a score above \(0.5\). The middle
range has lower Hits@10 and higher multiplicity than the zero-score range in
both models. However, the five relations with scores above \(0.5\) show a
markedly different result. For RotatE, their mean Hits@10 reaches \(0.8000\),
while both \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\) are \(0\). For TransE, their mean Hits@10
is \(0.6866\), while \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\) decrease to \(0.1361\) and
\(0.0813\), respectively. This is the highest Hits@10 and the lowest
multiplicity among the three score ranges for both models.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/chap5/inverse/score-groups.pdf}
    \caption{Relation-level results grouped by inverse clarity under
    \(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\). The label \emph{Score} in the table
    refers to the inverse clarity defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}.}
    \label{fig:inverse-score-groups}
\end{figure}

One possible interpretation is that partial or ambiguous reverse support is
not sufficient to provide a consistent paired constraint. When the inverse
score is high, the reverse evidence is concentrated on a strong and clearly
separated partner, which may reduce variation across repeated runs. This
interpretation is consistent with the sharp change in the highest score range,
but the current experiment does not test the underlying training mechanism.
Moreover, this range contains only five relations. The result therefore does
not establish that inverse scores provide a continuous benefit or that the same
cutoff will apply in other datasets. It shows that, in the current experiment,
the clearest positive inverse result is concentrated among relations with very
high inverse scores.

\subsection{Symmetry Analysis}
\label{sec:symmetry_analysis}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

For a symmetric relation, an entity pair can be connected by the same relation
in both directions. The two endpoint entities can therefore occupy compatible
roles under that relation, and the training graph provides structural evidence
for both directional orderings. This may reduce the distinction between the
head and tail roles and make predictions less sensitive to differences between
repeated runs.

As discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}, the raw symmetry score can be
inflated by self-loop triples because a self-loop is identical to its reversed
edge. The following analysis therefore uses the symmetry score that excludes
self-loops, \(\operatorname{Sym}^{\neg\mathrm{loop}}(r)\). The shorter label
\emph{Score} in the figures refers to this excluding-self-loop score.

\subsubsection{Results}

The global correlations in Figure~\ref{fig:symmetry-spearman} are weak. The
Spearman correlations with Hits@10 are \(0.0122\) for RotatE and \(0.0117\)
for TransE. The correlations with \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\) range from
\(0.0425\) to \(0.1310\). These results do not support a clear monotonic
association between the excluding-self-loop symmetry score and either
predictive performance or multiplicity.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/chap5/symmetry/spearman.pdf}
    \caption{Spearman correlations between the excluding-self-loop symmetry
    score and relation-level Hits@10, \(\alpha\), and \(\delta\) under
    \(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\).}
    \label{fig:symmetry-spearman}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:symmetry-score-groups} divides the 182 retained relations into
164 zero-score relations, six relations with scores in \((0,0.5]\), and 12
relations with scores above \(0.5\). Hits@10 shows the same non-monotonic
ordering for both models. The middle range has the lowest Hits@10, while the
high-score range recovers to a value slightly above the zero-score range. For
RotatE, the three means are \(0.5815\), \(0.5047\), and \(0.5924\). For
TransE, they are \(0.5605\), \(0.4725\), and \(0.5742\).

The multiplicity measures do not show the same cross-model pattern. For
TransE, \(\alpha\) decreases from \(0.4436\) in the middle range to \(0.3512\)
in the high-score range, and \(\delta\) decreases from \(0.2736\) to
\(0.2119\). Both values nevertheless remain above those of the zero-score
range. For RotatE, the high-score range does not show a corresponding
reduction: \(\alpha\) changes from \(0.3610\) to \(0.3735\), and \(\delta\)
changes from \(0.2236\) to \(0.2341\). Thus, the recovery in Hits@10 at high
symmetry scores does not translate into a consistent reduction in predictive
multiplicity.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/chap5/symmetry/score-groups.pdf}
    \caption{Relation-level results grouped by the excluding-self-loop
    symmetry score under \(\texttt{test\_support}\geq 10\).}
    \label{fig:symmetry-score-groups}
\end{figure}

Symmetry may provide coarse evidence that the two endpoints occupy compatible
roles, but it does not identify which particular entity should be ranked as the
correct answer. This distinction may explain why high symmetry is not
sufficient to produce a consistent stability benefit. However, it does not
explain why the six relations in the middle range have the lowest Hits@10. The
small size of this range also makes it difficult to interpret its mean as a
general penalty associated with weak symmetry. Therefore, the grouped results
should not be treated as evidence for a general U-shaped relationship.
Overall, under the current operationalization, symmetry does not provide a
clean standalone explanation of relation-level predictive multiplicity.
